# Case Study: Botspeak
## Teaching AI Fluency as a Tier 4 Capacity

*Category: Course Design / Tier 4 Metacognitive & Supervisory*
*Theory connection: The literacy/fluency distinction — why knowing which tools
to use is not the same as understanding what you are working with*

---

### The Causal Question

Can the gap between AI literacy (knowing how to use tools) and AI fluency
(understanding the cognitive nature of the entity you are collaborating with)
be closed through a designed curriculum — and if so, what does that curriculum
require?

---

### The Opening Case

A professional submits a report. It contains a citation to a study that does
not exist. The client's researcher finds it. The professional had no framework
for why it happened — and no framework means no defense, no correction, and no
way to prevent it next time.

This is not a broken tool story. It is not a story about a careless professional.
It is a story about the structural gap between literacy and fluency. The
professional was literate. She could generate outputs, prompt, and iterate. What
she could not do was evaluate what she was working with — because no one had ever
given her a framework for it. She was using a very sophisticated autocomplete and
thought she was using a reasoning partner.

That gap is where professional liability lives.

---

### The Literacy/Fluency Distinction

AI literacy is knowing which tools to use and how to operate them. It is
teachable in a day, platform-specific, and obsoletes rapidly as tools change.

AI fluency is understanding the cognitive nature of the entity being collaborated
with: what it does at superhuman level, what it cannot do at all, and what
remains irreducibly the human's. Fluency is not a harder version of literacy.
It is a different cognitive operation — one that maps directly onto Tier 4 of
the Irreducibly Human framework.

The professional who submitted the fabricated citation had Tier 1 access to a
Tier 1 tool and treated the output as if it had been produced by a Tier 5
reasoning process. She had no way to know the difference because she had never
been given the framework. The error was architectural, not behavioral.

The mechanism matters here. AI systems generate outputs through pattern
completion, not knowledge retrieval. This is not a subtle distinction. It means
that an AI's confidence of expression is structurally uncorrelated with the
accuracy of its content. The tool has reliability zones — domains near the center
of its training distribution where it is genuinely excellent, and edges where it
produces fluent, authoritative, wrong outputs with no internal signal that
anything has gone wrong.

Every ambiguity in a prompt is a decision the AI makes without the human. Most
professionals issuing prompts have never thought about what decisions they are
delegating or whether those decisions are theirs to delegate.

### The Course Architecture

Botspeak is a four-credit graduate seminar at Northeastern University, College
of Engineering, designed as the entry point to the Irreducibly Human series.
It has no technical prerequisites. It is explicitly for professionals who have
used AI tools but have never been given a framework for evaluating what they
are working with.

The course is structured in three acts tracking the arc from literacy to fluency
to original practice.

**Act One — Establish (Weeks 1–3)** builds survival vocabulary before introducing
framework. Two high-stakes failure cases establish the structural pattern of AI
failure before a single definition is offered. The Irreducibly Human tier taxonomy
and the Five Modes framework are introduced at the end of Act One — after students
have spent three weeks accumulating domain-specific evidence that the framework
is necessary. This sequencing is deliberate. Framework-first produces vocabulary
without intuition. Evidence-first produces the question that the framework
answers.

**Act Two — Build (Weeks 4–8)** teaches one mode per week, each introduced through
a failure case from a professional domain. The five modes are Specification,
Delegation, Conversation, Discernment, and Diligence — covering the full arc of
a human-AI collaboration from task definition through ongoing monitoring. Each
week requires application, not recitation. Act Two closes with a midterm
presenting a novel multi-mode case with no scaffolding and no annotation. All
five modes, applied as practices.

**Act Three — Apply (Weeks 9–15)** stops giving clean single-mode cases and moves
to the messy real conditions where fluency either holds or it doesn't. Trust
calibration across compounding multi-step workflows. Adversarial validation — the
verification mode designed to catch failures that ordinary review misses. Original
research as the terminal deliverable.

---

### The Five Modes

The Five Modes constitute the course's primary conceptual contribution. They are
not prompting tips. They are a complete architecture for AI collaboration.

**Specification** — the work that happens before any prompt is written. Every
ambiguity in a specification is a decision the AI makes without the human. The
five-component specification (intent, constraints, success criteria, exclusions,
output format) is not a template. It is a forcing function for making delegation
decisions explicit before they default to the tool.

**Delegation** — the explicit mapping of what is given to the AI and what is
retained. The course introduces the performance paradox: better short-term outputs
can produce long-term capability degradation when the human delegates herself out
of the cognitive work that builds expertise. The tier taxonomy is the instrument
for making delegation decisions: Tier 1 tasks can be fully delegated; Tier 7
tasks cannot be delegated at all; the interesting cases are in the middle.

**Conversation** — the adversarial mode. The failure case for this week is a policy
researcher who developed a polished, internally consistent argument over 45 minutes
of AI conversation. The strongest counterargument in her field never appeared.
She never asked. Her peer reviewer found it immediately. AI conversation defaults
toward agreement and polish. Fluency requires deliberately introducing friction —
steelmanning opposition, probing edge cases, surfacing assumptions. The adversarial
conversation workshop is the one session in the course with no substitute. It
cannot be performed by reading about it.

**Discernment** — the verification mode, built around four layers: fact, reasoning,
framing, and omission. The first three layers are what most professionals verify.
The fourth layer — what the AI did not include, could not include, or was
structurally incapable of including — is the layer most professionals never reach.
The failure case for this week is the pharmacist at the Human Decision Node: a
14-medication patient, compromised renal function, an AI interaction check that
returned "no significant interactions." Three options: accept, reject, or discern.
The course teaches Option 3.

**Diligence** — the ongoing monitoring mode after deployment. The failure case is
Amazon's recruiting algorithm: not why it was biased, but why no one caught it for
a year. The accountability chain was intact on paper and absent in practice. Three
forms of AI degradation (model drift, context drift, use case drift) and three
ways accountability gets obscured (process laundering, tool diffusion, verification
gap) are introduced as the structural explanation for how well-intentioned
deployments produce harms nobody designed.

### The Assessment Architecture

The course's assessment design is the most direct expression of the Irreducibly
Human argument in pedagogical form.

AI tools are available, expected, and explicitly permitted on every assignment.
The assessment is not whether students used AI. The assessment is the layer AI
cannot perform.

Every assignment requires an AI Use Disclosure. Not as compliance — as the
course's primary assessment instrument. Students document what they used, how
they used it, what they changed, and — this field is not optional — what the AI
could not do. Specifically: at least one judgment call that required the student's
values, domain knowledge, or professional accountability. A disclosure that cannot
name one such judgment call has not demonstrated that the student performed the
irreducibly human layer.

The terminal deliverable is a complete original research project in AI fluency,
with a full iteration log and a verification record. The section that carries half
the grade is not the research findings. It is the Irreducibly Human accounting:
three specific judgment calls that required human values, domain knowledge, or
accountability; one judgment call that was tried-as-delegation and then reclaimed;
and an honest assessment of the collaboration — where the AI was genuinely useful,
where it produced confident-sounding noise, and what the student would do
differently.

The assessment architecture operationalizes the argument. Every student in the
course is required, at least once per assignment, to name what the tool could not
do. That practice — the habit of locating the human's specific contribution — is
the Tier 4 capacity the course develops. Not the knowledge of which tools to use.
The judgment about where the tool ends and the human begins.

### The Tier Mapping

The course is explicitly designed around Tier 4 (Metacognitive & Supervisory) as
its primary developmental target, with Tier 5 (Causal & Counterfactual) as a
secondary target in Act Three.

**Tier 1** is the entry state. Students arrive with Tier 1 access to AI tools.
The course opens by demonstrating why Tier 1 access without Tier 4 oversight is
professionally dangerous.

**Tier 4** is where the Five Modes operate. Specification is problem formulation —
the Tier 4 capacity that requires understanding what problem is worth solving
before delegating its solution. Delegation is tool orchestration — knowing which
tool to use, when, and whether to trust it. Discernment is plausibility auditing —
the capacity to evaluate outputs one could not oneself have produced. Diligence is
interpretive judgment and ongoing executive oversight.

**Tier 5** emerges in the adversarial validation week (Week 11). The failure case —
a Phase III clinical trial failure tracing to a Phase II framing error that no
fact-check would have caught — introduces causal reasoning as the capacity
required to distinguish framing failures from factual errors. Verification at the
fact layer misses framing failures because they are not factually wrong. They are
wrong at the level of what question was asked. Causal reasoning is what asks
whether the question was the right question.

**Tier 7** appears in the capstone but is not formally taught. The judgment calls
that carried 50% of the final grade — the ones that required the student's values,
domain knowledge, or accountability — are Tier 7 in practice. The course does not
claim to teach practical wisdom. It claims to create the conditions under which
students encounter its requirements: real stakes, genuine uncertainty, no rubric
that resolves the decision.

---

### What the Course Claims and What It Doesn't

The course claims to develop Tier 4 AI fluency in graduate professionals who
arrive with Tier 1 literacy. The assessment architecture — AI Use Disclosures as
primary instrument, Irreducibly Human section carrying half the capstone grade —
is designed to make that development visible and evaluable.

The course does not claim to develop domain expertise. It assumes students arrive
with domain expertise and teaches them to bring that expertise to bear on AI
collaboration rather than delegating themselves out of it.

The course does not claim to solve the AI ethics problem. The Diligence mode
teaches students to notice when accountability has been obscured. It does not
teach them what to do when the institutional incentives point toward obscuring it.

The course does not claim that the Five Modes will remain the right framework as
AI capabilities change. It claims that the underlying cognitive architecture —
the move from literacy to fluency, from tool use to judgment about tool use —
is the stable target regardless of which specific tools exist. Students who
complete the course with Tier 4 fluency around 2026 AI tools should be able to
apply that fluency to tools that do not yet exist. That generalization claim has
not been validated longitudinally. It is the course's central architectural bet.

---

### The Connection to the Broader Argument

The fabricated citation that opened this case study is a Tier 1 tool producing
a Tier 1 output — plausible pattern completion — in a context that required a Tier
4 user to evaluate it. The professional had the literacy to generate the output.
She lacked the fluency to know she needed to check it, how to check it, and what
checking it would require.

That gap is what the factory model curriculum produces at scale. Twelve years of
training students to produce correct outputs on well-specified problems, with no
training in evaluating whether the outputs are correct, whether the problems were
well-specified, or whether the specification was worth the effort. AI collapsed
the economic value of the output layer and left the evaluation layer exposed.

Botspeak is an explicit intervention at that exposure. It is the microscope
training that should have accompanied the microscope.
