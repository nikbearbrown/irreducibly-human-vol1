# Case Study: Conducting AI
## Building the Five Supervisory Capacities No Algorithm Possesses

*Category: Course Design / Tier 4 Metacognitive & Supervisory (Advanced)*
*Theory connection: The solve-verify asymmetry — why AI capability scaling
deepens rather than closes the human supervisory requirement, and what it takes
to develop that capacity under adversarial conditions*

---

### The Causal Question

When graduate engineers can operate AI tools fluently but cannot explain why
their outputs should be trusted, what specific capacities are missing — and
can they be developed deliberately?

---

### The Conductor

A conductor does not play any instrument.

They hold the whole performance in mind while each section plays its part. They
hear the wrong note before the score confirms it. They decide which piece is
worth performing and how it should be interpreted. The performance collapses
without them even though they produce no sound themselves.

This is what graduate-level AI supervision looks like. And it is the role
that every AI integration program currently fails to develop.

Graduate engineers learn to use AI tools. They learn to prompt, delegate, and
verify outputs. They become, by any reasonable measure, competent. Then they
encounter a situation where something feels wrong before they can prove it.
Where the problem they have been handed is the wrong problem. Where the tool
is producing a result that is accurate, efficient, and pointed in the wrong
direction — and they have no framework for what to do. They have learned to
play their instrument. Nobody taught them to conduct.

---

### The Structural Argument

The course's central claim is not philosophical. It is mathematical.

AI systems solve faster than any human. That gap will not close — it will widen.
What will not change is the solve-verify asymmetry: AI optimizes for the common
and the likely. It cannot verify whether its output is grounded in the specific
domain reality at hand. It cannot reframe a poorly formulated problem. It cannot
interpret what an accurate output means in a specific human context. It cannot
integrate multiple legitimate but conflicting perspectives into a recommendation
that someone is accountable for.

These are not limitations that better models will eventually close. They are
structural features of what statistical pattern matching is. As AI capability
scales, the human supervisory role does not diminish. It becomes more
consequential. The conductor's work becomes harder, not easier, as the orchestra
gets faster.

This is the asymmetry that Botspeak introduces and that Conducting AI builds
into operational capacity. The professional who verifies in a high-velocity AI
workflow is doing the consequential work, not the diminished work.

### The Five Capacities

The course develops five supervisory capacities, named as the conductor's
specific cognitive acts:

**Capacity 1 — Plausibility Auditing.** The judgment that happens before
verification — determining whether an output needs scrutiny and what to look
for when it does. Not fact-checking. Not error detection. The prior judgment
that either licenses trust or triggers investigation. Built from two mechanisms:
domain-grounded pattern recognition (the experienced practitioner's sense that
something doesn't fit the domain's behavior) and anomaly detection against a
mental model of how the relevant system works.

The diagnostic case is the ARC puzzle: GPT-4 scores at the 90th percentile on
medical licensing exams and cannot solve a puzzle a five-year-old can. Statistical
likelihood across a training corpus is not the same as a grounded model of how
the world works. The plausibility auditor's skill is exactly the gap between
those two things.

**Capacity 2 — Problem Formulation.** Reframing toward the salient and important
before AI engagement — not after. The Semmelweis case establishes the argument:
the formulation that saves lives was not the computationally tractable one. AI
optimizes for the common and likely. The human must reframe toward the important.

The co-evolutionary model of problem and solution — where problems and solutions
develop together through reflective practice — explains why handing problem
definition to an AI system is not delegation but abdication. The problem-solution
fit that a good practitioner finds through iteration is not available to a system
that cannot evaluate what matters in the domain. Handing the problem to the tool
before formulating it is the professional equivalent of asking the orchestra to
choose the piece.

**Capacity 3 — Tool Orchestration.** Selecting and sequencing tools for what
the procedure requires at this step, with every handoff and trust decision
explicit. The surgical instrument selection metaphor is precise: a surgeon
selects instruments based on what the procedure requires at this step, not
on what is already in hand. The sequencing principle that emerges from the
course: use one tool to audit another's output, selecting audit tools with
different failure modes so they catch each other's blind spots. Every trust
decision — the moment of accepting one tool's output as input to the next step —
is documented and defensible.

**Capacity 4 — Interpretive Judgment.** Supplying moral and cognitive legitimacy
to an AI output — the two types AI does not achieve. The three legitimacy types:
pragmatic (does it work efficiently), moral (is it aligned with human values),
cognitive (is it transparent and trustworthy). AI achieves pragmatic legitimacy
readily. Humans must supply the other two.

The same chord in two contexts — a nursery rhyme and a funeral march. Identical
notes. The conductor is the source of the difference. An AI analysis produces
findings. The professional who signs the recommendation has determined what those
findings mean in this context, for these people, with these stakes. That
determination is interpretive judgment. No audit of the analysis can substitute
for it.

**Capacity 5 — Executive Integration.** Not sequencing the four prior capacities
but holding all four simultaneously toward a unified goal — recognizing when one
raises a concern that requires another to re-engage. A quarterback who can throw,
read defenses, manage the clock, and lead a huddle has four distinct skills.
A quarterback who has never done all four simultaneously in a fourth-quarter
drive has not yet developed executive integration.

The urban planning case makes this visible: four AI tools, four technically
correct outputs, no integration, a wrong performance. The performance was wrong
not because any individual tool failed but because no one held the whole in mind
while the parts were playing.

### The Course Architecture

Conducting AI is a four-credit graduate seminar at Northeastern University,
College of Engineering. It requires Botspeak or equivalent as a prerequisite.
It is the advanced Tier 4 course in the series — building on the fluency
framework Botspeak establishes and developing it into operational supervisory
capacity under adversarial conditions.

**Act One — The Conductorless Orchestra (Weeks 1–3)** opens with five cases
and no framework. An engineer, a physician, a lawyer, a financial analyst, a
logistics manager — each using AI tools competently, each producing an output
that is plausible, confident, and consequentially wrong, each a failure the
tools did not cause and the human did not catch. After the fifth case, one
question: what was missing in every one of these situations?

The conductor metaphor earns its place rather than being declared. The Gardnerian
Gap is named: Gardner catalogued the instruments human minds can play; nobody
named the metacognitive capacity to direct them toward a unified purpose. In the
age of AI, every instrument has been augmented. The conductor has not. Act One
closes with the student's personal AI usage inventory — a self-assessment of
current practice at three diagnostic levels — returned in Week 15 to document
what has changed.

**Act Two — The Five Capacities (Weeks 4–13)** develops one capacity per
two-week sequence: a framework week followed by an application week. Each
capacity is introduced through a case that makes its absence felt before its
structure is named. The application weeks open with the assessment instrument
before any instruction — a deliberate reversal that forces students to apply
the capacity from current understanding before the framework is taught.

The Act Two arc builds deliberately. Plausibility auditing is the first capacity
because it is the prerequisite for all others — without the prior judgment that
licenses or triggers scrutiny, verification is either absent or arbitrary.
Problem formulation is second because it is upstream of tool selection —
choosing the right tools for the wrong problem is orchestration without purpose.
Tool orchestration third because it is where the handoff decisions live.
Interpretive judgment fourth because it is where the output returns to the
human. Executive integration last because it requires all four of the prior
capacities to have been developed before they can be held simultaneously.

Act Two closes with the supervisory analysis: a complete six-section document
demonstrating all five capacities on a real problem from the student's own
domain. This document carries forward into the adversarial audit.

**Act Three — The Full Performance (Weeks 14–15)** is peer critique followed
by the Plausibility Audit. The peer critique applies the five-capacity framework
as a structured evaluation instrument — specific evidence required for every
finding. Before submitting to the Plausibility Auditor, the student predicts
the three most likely audit findings. That prediction is graded against what
the auditor actually finds.

The Plausibility Audit is the course's terminal assessment and its most
structurally novel feature. Claude functions as the Plausibility Auditor,
applying the five-capacity framework adversarially to the student's supervisory
analysis — looking for undocumented problem formulation handoffs, orchestration
steps without verification, interpretive judgment that diagnoses legitimacy gaps
without filling them, and integration sections that sequence rather than
integrate. The student evaluates each finding: genuine failure or false positive,
with reasoning.

Then the Gap Account: a written account of at least one failure mode the auditor
could not detect, with a specific explanation of why detecting it required human
supervisory judgment that no prompt can supply. The student whose revised analysis
has no genuine findings has demonstrated all five capacities under adversarial
conditions.

The course closes with the Week 1 personal case inventory returned. Three
specific judgment calls the student now makes that they would have delegated,
deferred, or missed at the start of the semester. Named. Specific.

---

### The Assessment Architecture

Every deliverable requires what the course calls the Assessment Spine: a written
statement naming at least one judgment call that required the student's values,
domain knowledge, or accountability — that an AI could not have made on their
behalf. Not optional. Not a reflection prompt. The course's proof-of-concept,
repeated across every submission.

The terminal assessment — the Plausibility Audit, Gap Account, and Closing
Reflection — carries 250 points. The Gap Account is the most revealing element:
it requires the student to name what the adversarial auditor could not find and
explain why human supervisory capacity was required to find it. A student who
cannot produce this account has not yet understood what the course was developing.

### Botspeak and Conducting AI: The Same Tier, Different Depth

Conducting AI is Tier 4, like Botspeak. The difference is not in tier placement
but in operational depth. Botspeak develops the five modes as a framework for
understanding what AI is and how to work with it. Conducting AI develops five
specific capacities as operational practices demonstrated under adversarial
conditions.

The Botspeak graduate understands plausibility auditing conceptually. The
Conducting AI graduate has performed a plausibility audit on a real problem in
their domain, submitted it to an adversarial auditor, evaluated the findings,
and produced a Gap Account explaining what the auditor could not find. The
difference between understanding and operational capacity is the gap the course
closes.

This maps onto the Dreyfus model directly. Botspeak moves students from Novice
toward Advanced Beginner — they have the vocabulary and the framework. Conducting
AI moves from Advanced Beginner toward Competent and Proficient — they have
applied the framework under real conditions and developed the contextual judgment
that competence requires.

### Three Supervisory Judgments Only the Human Makes

**The prior plausibility judgment.** Before any verification check, the
experienced practitioner forms a judgment about whether an output warrants
scrutiny — and what to look for if it does. This judgment is domain-grounded
pattern recognition: the output does not fit how this domain behaves. The AI
cannot make this judgment because the judgment depends on a model of domain
behavior that comes from years of practice in that domain. An AI trained on
domain literature has statistical knowledge about that domain. The practitioner
has structural knowledge — a mental model of how the domain's systems actually
work that licenses anomaly detection.

**The reframe decision.** The problem as given is a hypothesis. Before engaging
any tool, the practitioner decides whether the hypothesis is the right one —
whether the brief is asking the question worth answering. This decision requires
knowing what matters in the domain, what the real stakes are, and whether the
framing serves the person with the problem or the person who formulated it. The
Semmelweis case demonstrates what happens when the reframe is not made: the
computationally tractable formulation is pursued, the lives-saving formulation
is missed, and the data confirms the wrong theory efficiently.

**The accountability commitment.** The interpretive judgment that supplies moral
and cognitive legitimacy to an AI output ends with a professional signing their
name to a recommendation. That signature means something the AI's output does
not: the professional is accountable for what the recommendation causes. The
accountability is not a formality. It is the condition under which rigorous
verification and honest interpretation become worth doing. An AI system is not
accountable for its outputs in any sense that creates an incentive to get them
right. The professional who signs the memo is.

### The Solve-Verify Asymmetry in Full

The course's central structural argument deserves direct statement because it
is the claim that most AI integration discourse either misses or reverses.

As AI capability scales, the solve side of the solve-verify asymmetry gets
faster, broader, and more powerful. The tools generate more outputs, across
more domains, with higher polish, at greater speed. Every dimension of generation
improves continuously.

The verify side does not scale in the same way. Verification requires exactly
the capacities the course develops: the domain-grounded plausibility judgment
that no training corpus can substitute for, the problem formulation that requires
knowing what matters in the specific context, the interpretive judgment that
supplies the two legitimacy types AI does not achieve, the executive integration
that holds the whole performance in mind while the parts play. These capacities
are human in origin, domain-specific in development, and slow to build. They do
not improve by adding compute.

The professional who can generate without verifying is more dangerous as the
tools get better — because the outputs are more polished, more voluminous, and
more confidently wrong in subtler ways. The professional who can verify is more
valuable as the tools get better — because the verification task is harder, the
stakes are higher, and fewer people have the developed capacity to perform it.

This is the argument the course teaches students to make — and to demonstrate,
under adversarial conditions, that they can perform.

### What the Course Claims and What It Doesn't

The course claims that the five supervisory capacities can be developed
deliberately, that the development is assessable under adversarial conditions,
and that a student who has completed the full pipeline — supervisory analysis,
peer critique, Plausibility Audit, Gap Account — has demonstrated operational
capacity that cannot be faked by fluent AI use.

The course does not claim that the five capacities are exhaustive. They are
the five that the solve-verify asymmetry makes most consequential in
AI-assisted professional work. A domain may require additional supervisory
capacities specific to its nature.

The course does not claim that completing Conducting AI produces executive
integration at the expert level. The Dreyfus model is clear that expertise
requires years of contextual experience beyond what any course can provide.
The course claims to move students from Advanced Beginner toward Competent and
Proficient — giving them the framework and the first adversarial proof that the
framework is operational.

The course does not claim that the Plausibility Auditor architecture (Claude
functioning as the adversarial assessor) is a final solution to the assessment
problem. It is a novel structural design whose validity depends on whether
the adversarial audit is actually challenging — whether the AI finds real
failures in insufficiently developed supervisory analyses. That depends on the
quality of the audit prompt architecture, which is the most technically fragile
element of the course design.

---

### The Connection to the Broader Argument

The conductorless orchestra is the factory model's legacy applied to AI-assisted
professional work. Train for instrument proficiency. Deploy in an ensemble.
Assume someone is conducting. Nobody is.

Every professional who learned to use AI tools without learning to supervise
their outputs is a musician playing their part with no sense of the whole
performance. They are competent. They are not sufficient. The performance
requires a conductor — and the conductor's five capacities are what the factory
model never taught, because the factory model was optimizing for instrument
proficiency, not ensemble judgment.

The solve-verify asymmetry is not a threat to be managed. It is the structural
reason that human supervisory capacity becomes more valuable, not less, as AI
capability scales. The conductor is not replaced by a better orchestra. The
conductor is required by it.
