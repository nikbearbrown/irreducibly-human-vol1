# Case Study: AImagineering
## Teaching the Design Pipeline When Making Is No Longer the Bottleneck

*Category: Course Design / Tiers 3, 4, 7*
*Theory connection: What happens when the constraint that forced design courses
to spend eighty percent of their time on production collapses — and what was
always the harder work underneath it becomes visible*

---

### The Causal Question

When AI eliminates production time as the bottleneck in design, what remains
that is irreducibly human — and can it be taught as a deliberate sequence?

---

### The Dirty Secret

Design thinking courses have always had a dirty secret: most of the semester
is spent making the artifact.

Sketching, prototyping, iterating — the physical production of a thing to test
with a user. The cognitive work of design — understanding who has the problem,
deciding which problem is worth solving, choosing among fifty plausible
directions, committing to one with your name on the consequences — gets
compressed into the margins. Not because it is less important. Because making
was hard, and it consumed the time.

AI eliminated that excuse.

A brief that produced a working prototype in a week now produces one in an
afternoon. Thirty concept sketches that once required a sprint now require a
prompt. The constraint that forced design courses to spend eighty percent of
their time on production has collapsed — and it has exposed what was always
the harder work underneath it. That work is not making. It is thinking.

This is not straightforwardly a gift. When ideation is a tool operation rather
than a cognitive achievement, the discipline required to do it well changes
entirely. The engineer who can generate fifty concepts in an afternoon now faces
a problem the design sprint methodology was not built to address: how do you
choose? Not which concept is most polished — the tool makes them all polished.
Which concept is worth building. Which problem was worth solving in the first
place. Whether the brief you were handed was even asking the right question.

And at the end, when you have built and tested something: what do you actually
commit to, and what are you accountable for if it fails?

---

### The Missing Stage

Design Thinking as typically taught has five stages: Empathize, Define, Ideate,
Prototype, Test. The sequence ends at Test. Prototype, iterate, test — and then,
implicitly, hand the results to someone else.

That omission was tolerable when the pipeline took a semester. It is not
tolerable when AI compresses the pipeline to three weeks and leaves the human
with nothing to do but make decisions.

The missing stage is the **Commit**: a specific course of action, with its
evidential basis stated, its uncertainty acknowledged, and its accountability
owned. Not a recommendation. Not a direction. Not a set of options. A decision.

No AI tool makes a Commit. The tool can generate options, evaluate tradeoffs,
synthesize test results, and produce fluent summaries of what the evidence
shows. What it cannot do is stake its name on one of the options, acknowledge
what it does not know, and accept accountability for what happens next. Those
operations require a self that persists through time and has something to lose.

The course exists to teach the Commit — and everything before it that makes
the Commit possible.

### The Course Architecture

AImagineering is a four-credit graduate seminar at Northeastern University,
College of Engineering. It requires Botspeak or equivalent as a prerequisite —
students must already understand the difference between pattern completion and
knowledge retrieval, and must have experience directing AI tools at the
specification-and-delegation level. The course builds on that fluency rather
than establishing it.

The full pipeline is: **Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test, Commit.**
The sixth stage is the course's intellectual contribution over standard
Design Thinking.

**Act One — The Material and the Human (Weeks 1–7)** is everything before a
concept is generated. It opens with an experiment: thirty minutes, a brief,
AI tools open. The output is good. Session B names what was assumed — a
taxonomy of the human judgment the session skipped: about the user, the
problem, the context, the values at stake, and what would happen if the output
failed. The act then builds the capacities that need to happen before the tool
runs.

**Finding the grain** (Weeks 2–3) introduces the concept of tool affordances
and resistances. A community health intervention designed entirely with a
language model produces communication that is coherent, complete, and addressed
to the community rather than from it. The grain explains this: every AI tool
does some things naturally and resists others. Working against the grain without
knowing it produces the wrong kind of output. Students map the grain of their
primary tool against their capstone domain brief. The grain is not a technical
specification. It is a working knowledge of what the tool treats as normal —
and what that means for the human judgment required to use it well.

**What simulation cannot feel** (Weeks 4–5) places an AI-generated user persona
next to a field note from an actual conversation. They are not the same document.
AI personas are trained on population patterns — they produce plausible users,
not specific ones. The empathy investigation protocol goes where simulation
cannot: the unexpected, the contradictory, the embodied. Students conduct a
genuine empathy investigation with at least two human contacts and identify at
least one finding that would not appear in any AI-generated persona for their
user group. The finding is not evidence that AI personas are useless. It is
evidence that plausible is not the same as true, and that design for specific
people in specific contexts requires contact with those people in those contexts.

**The brief is a hypothesis** (Weeks 6–7) names the reframe as a values decision.
"Design a faster horse" generates a very different AI output than "help people
move between places efficiently." The choice of brief is a human judgment made
before any tool runs — a decision about what problem is worth solving that no
algorithm can make because the algorithm does not know what the designer cares
about. Students produce three alternative problem definitions for their capstone
brief, evaluate each against explicit criteria, and defend a selection in a
live three-minute presentation that fields peer objection. Act One closes with
the midterm: a novel brief, no scaffolding, all four pre-ideation capacities
demonstrated as practice.

**Act Two — The Human Capacities (Weeks 8–13)** moves through the pipeline
stages where AI assists but does not decide.

The ideation week — called the Dreamer's week — is the only week where AI
owns the session. Students direct an AI ideation session producing at least
thirty concepts, then face the harder problem: curation. Choosing among fifty
plausible outputs requires criteria, and criteria require judgment. Why do the
surprising concepts survive when the plausible ones shouldn't? The three
concepts that survive require a 300-word written defense of each selection. The
Dreamer's week is the course's most direct engagement with the Tier 1 trap: AI
made ideation a tool operation. That is not the same as making it
a cognitive achievement. The judgment that makes ideation valuable — the
selection, the defense, the commitment to a direction — remains with the human.

The prototype build (Weeks 9–11) requires students to write a complete
specification before the tool runs, making every identification decision
explicit. Which metric is most motivating? Where does it surface in the user
journey? What happens when a user sees demoralizing data? These are judgment
calls that shape what gets built. None of them are generated by the tool
without the human putting them in the specification first.

The testing phase (Weeks 12–13) introduces the three legitimacy types —
pragmatic (does it work), moral (should it exist), cognitive (can it be
trusted). AI achieves pragmatic legitimacy readily. It does not adjudicate
the other two. Test results answer pragmatic questions. Whether the thing
should exist in the world, and whether users can trust it, are questions the
data surfaces but does not answer. That interpretation is the human's work.

**Act Three — The Commit (Weeks 14–15)** is the stage Design Thinking omits.
It was invisible when prototyping was hard. AI has made the omission visible
and, for engineers who work with AI-accelerated pipelines, increasingly costly.

The Commit document has five required elements. The decision — a specific course
of action, not a recommendation. The evidence — direct citations from the test
phase. The uncertainty — what is not known before deployment. The accountability
— what the designer is responsible for if it fails. The revision condition —
what new information would change the decision.

A Commit document that cannot name what the designer does not know has not
demonstrated genuine accountability — it has produced the appearance of
commitment while retaining the option to disclaim responsibility later. A Commit
document that cannot name the revision condition has not demonstrated genuine
reasoning — it has produced a position that cannot be updated. Both failures
are common in professional contexts. Both are identifiable by a peer reviewer.
The supervised peer critique is mandatory because it cannot be performed
by reading about it.

### The Assessment Architecture

Every assignment requires an AI Use Disclosure naming at least one judgment
call that required the student's values, domain knowledge, or professional
accountability. The Irreducibly Human section of the final pipeline presentation
carries fifty percent of the final project grade.

The full pipeline presentation is assessed as a narrative of judgment — not
the output, but every human decision from brief to Commit, in sequence, with
the metacognitive switches named. Three specific judgment calls that required
values, domain knowledge, or accountability. One judgment call that was
tried-as-delegation and then reclaimed. An honest assessment of the
collaboration — where AI was genuinely useful, where it produced
confident-sounding noise, and what the student would do differently.

The pipeline graduation statement closes the presentation — a single sentence
the student can stand behind as a description of what they can now do. The
instruction is earned, not recited.

### The Tier Mapping

AImagineering operates across three tiers simultaneously, which makes it the
most integrative course in the series.

**Tier 3 (Social & Personal)** is engaged in the empathy investigation. The
finding that would not appear in any AI-generated persona is evidence of
genuine contact with specific people in specific contexts — not population-level
pattern recognition, but the particular human encounter that changes the design.
The brief reframe is also a Tier 3 act: it requires the designer to inhabit the
perspective of someone whose problem is being solved, and to question whether
the framing of that problem serves the person or the framing's author.

**Tier 4 (Metacognitive & Supervisory)** is engaged throughout Act Two and
explicitly in the Commit. The grain mapping is Tier 4: understanding what a
tool does and does not do is the plausibility auditing function applied to
design tools. The curation of ideation outputs is Tier 4: knowing which
of fifty plausible concepts is worth building requires judgment about what the
problem requires, not just what the tool produced. The Commit is Tier 4 at
its most demanding: problem formulation (what is the decision), interpretive
judgment (what does the evidence support), tool orchestration (what role did
each tool play), and accountability for the result.

**Tier 7 (Existential & Wisdom)** is what the Commit requires and the course
cannot fully teach. Practical wisdom — Aristotle's phronesis — requires
knowing when and how to apply what you know, and when not to. It requires
stakes, the possibility of loss. The Commit document, with its accountability
element and its revision condition, is designed to create the conditions under
which Tier 7 judgment becomes visible: real stakes, genuine uncertainty, no
rubric that resolves the decision. The course does not claim to teach practical
wisdom. It claims to create the conditions under which a student first
encounters its requirements.

### Three Judgment Calls Only the Human Makes

**The reframe.** "Design a faster horse" is not the same problem as "help people
move between places efficiently." The choice between them is a values decision
about whose interests the design serves, what the designer believes the user
actually needs, and whether the brief as given was asking the right question.
The AI generates excellent responses to both briefs. It cannot evaluate which
brief is worth answering. That evaluation requires the designer to know something
about the domain, the user, and what she cares about that the tool does not
have access to.

**The selection from ideation.** Thirty AI-generated concepts, each plausible,
each polished, each addressing the brief. Which three survive? The criteria that
answer this question are not in the brief. They are in the designer's judgment
about what the problem requires, what the user can use, what is buildable in
context, and what is worth the accountability of committing to. The tool
generated the space. The human navigates it. That navigation is the design work.

**The accountability element.** The Commit document requires the designer to
state what she is responsible for if it fails. Not what the tool produced. Not
what the test results showed. What she, specifically, is accountable for if
the design is deployed and causes harm, or fails to deliver, or produces
outcomes the test did not surface. No AI tool makes this statement. The
statement is meaningless without a self that persists through time and has
something to lose. That is the Commit.

### What the Course Claims and What It Doesn't

The course claims that the pre-ideation and post-test stages of design —
the stages AI cannot perform — can be taught as a deliberate sequence, and
that the Commit is not a harder version of the prototype review but a
different professional operation requiring a different kind of thinking.

The course does not claim that AI ideation is useless. The Dreamer's week
exists because AI-accelerated ideation is genuinely valuable. The course's
argument is not against the tool. It is about what happens before the tool
runs and after the tool finishes — and that those stages, not the generation
stage, are now where design competence lives.

The course does not claim that the Commit always produces the right decision.
It claims that the Commit produces an honest decision — one whose evidence
is stated, whose uncertainty is acknowledged, and whose accountability is
owned. That is different from a confident decision, and substantially more
defensible in the contexts where design decisions have real consequences.

The course does not claim to teach Tier 7 wisdom. It claims to create the
first conditions under which students encounter its requirements. Whether a
student develops practical wisdom from those conditions depends on what they
do with them after the course ends.

---

### The Connection to the Broader Argument

The design thinking curriculum's dirty secret — eighty percent of the semester
on production, twenty percent on thinking — was tolerable because production
was hard. AI made production easy. The eighty percent collapsed. What remained
was the twenty percent that was always the harder work and was always the part
that mattered.

Every design student who emerged from the standard curriculum knowing how to
prototype and not knowing how to commit was a microscope sitting in a box.
Capable of producing. Unable to decide.

The AImagineering course is the intervention at the commitment layer — the place
where the design ends and the designer's accountability begins. The pipeline
graduation statement is not the final product. It is what the designer can say,
in one sentence, about what she can do that the tool cannot.

That sentence is the point.
